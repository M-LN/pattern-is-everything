The Learning Duck

3 learners · runs in your browser
◆ The PatternA learner can only learn what its senses and its reward let it see

A duck has to find bread. Nobody tells it how. On this page the same small problem is solved three times, by three kinds of learning: evolution, a table of experience, and a neural network. All three run live in your browser. You can watch what each one learns, and you can break each one.

The three are not toys of three different fields. They are the ladder that reinforcement learning climbed, from selection to tables to the deep networks that learned to play Atari games and that walking robots are trained with today.

Lab 1

Evolution: learning without a learner

Thirty ducks start with random brains. Each brain is a tiny neural network of 68 numbers that turns what the duck senses into a turn and a speed. A duck that eats enough has ducklings, and each duckling gets a copy of its mother's brain with small random changes. A pike hunts them all.

No duck ever learns anything during its life. The population does.

Every four seconds of pond time each brain gets a small test: bread on its left, then on its right, and the same with the pike. A random brain passes about a quarter of the time. Try 25× and two pikes.

◆ Selection is a search algorithm. Mutation proposes, survival decides, and what is learned ends up stored in the population, not in any single duck. The same loop drives genetic algorithms and neuroevolution. It needs no gradient and no reward signal after each move, only a way to tell who did better.
Lab 2

Q-learning: a table of experience

Now one duck learns during its own life. It splits the world into 36 situations: where the bread is (12 directions) and how far away it is (3 rings). For each situation it keeps a score for each of five moves, from hard left to hard right. After every move it nudges that score toward what actually happened. That is Q-learning.

Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α · [ r + γ · maxa′ Q(s′, a′) − Q(s, a) ] α = 0.2, how fast old scores give way. γ = 0.9, how much the future counts. r is the reward: a little for getting closer to the bread, a lot for reaching it, a small cost for every step. The bracket is the surprise: what happened minus what the duck expected.

◆ The sim-to-real gap. A policy is only as good as the world it was trained in. In real water a straight move drifts to the right, so the trained duck circles the bread. With learning on it recovers, and the compass shows how: the blue cells turn orange, because the duck finds it is quicker to go round to the right than to fight its weak left turn. Robot builders fight the same gap when a walking policy trained in a physics simulator meets a real floor.
Lab 3

Deep Q-network: a neural network instead of a table

A table cannot cope with reeds. There are far too many situations to list. So the third duck replaces the table with a small neural network of 988 numbers. Its inputs are seven eyes that measure the distance to reeds, a nose that smells the bread, and a sense of touch. Its outputs are scores for four moves: left, straight, right and back up. It learns with the same update as the table, from a memory of its last 20,000 moves.

The smell spreads around obstacles the way it would in water, so the nose points along the shortest open path. The yellow glow on the water is that smell.

◆ Generalisation. The network never stores a situation. It learns a function from senses to scores, so it can act sensibly in a layout it has never seen. Train it in Reeds, switch learning off, then choose Bridge or draw your own reeds. If it still finds the bread, it has learned the principle and not the map.

Three failures you can reproduce

Each lab on this page was built by watching a duck fail. These three failures are the ones that matter far beyond ducks.

Reward hacking: you get what you reward, not what you meant

The first version of Lab 3 gave the duck a nose that pointed straight at the bread and paid it for every step that brought it closer in a straight line. It learned to press itself against the reed nearest the bread and stay there, because the long way round first makes the distance worse. Nothing was wrong with the learning. The reward described a different goal than the one intended.

See it: in Lab 3, choose Bridge, switch on Straight-line nose and press Forget everything. In our test runs it found about 2 pieces of bread per 1,000 steps and hit the reeds about 40 times. With the smell that flows around obstacles it found about 5 and hit them about 3 times. The fix was to change what is rewarded, not how the duck learns.

The sim-to-real gap: the world changed, the policy did not

A policy learns the world it is trained in, including its accidents. Move it to a slightly different world and it fails in ways that look strange from outside but are perfectly consistent from inside.

See it: in Lab 2, train to over 90% in simulation, turn learning off and switch to real water. In our runs it dropped to between 20% and 35%. With learning back on it recovered to about 65%. In data work the same thing is called distribution shift.

Partial observability: you cannot learn what you cannot sense

The duck in Lab 3 only knows this one moment. It cannot tell where fleeing bread is heading, and its eyes report a pike exactly as they report a reed. More practice does not help, because the information is not in its inputs. The fix is a new sense or a memory, for example a recurrent network that carries the last few seconds forward.

See it: in Lab 3, switch on Fleeing bread or Pike after the duck has learned. The Taken counter keeps rising and the bread count stays low, however long it trains.

Method

Lab 1
Each brain has 9 inputs (direction and distance to the nearest bread and pike, a wall ahead, its own energy, a constant), 6 hidden tanh neurons and 2 outputs (turn and speed). Food gives energy, swimming costs energy, and a duck with enough energy has a duckling. Each of the 68 weights mutates with probability 0.12 by a Gaussian step of 0.3. If fewer than six ducks remain, newcomers are mutated copies of the best duck so far.
Lab 2
Tabular Q-learning with 36 states and 5 actions, α = 0.2, γ = 0.9. Exploration starts at 30% random moves and decays by 1.5% per try to 3%. Reward per step: −0.02, plus 0.12 per pixel closer, plus 5 for the bread. Real water adds a current of (0.5, 0.2) pixels per step, a turning bias of 0.13 radians per step and a little noise.
Lab 3
Double DQN with a 11–24–24–4 ReLU network, Huber loss and Adam (learning rate 0.0005), batch 32, replay memory 20,000, target network synced every 600 updates, γ = 0.95. Exploration falls from 100% to 4% over 25,000 steps. Reward per step: −0.01, plus 0.06 per pixel closer along the smell, −1 for touching a reed, +5 for the bread, −3 if the pike takes it. The smell is a shortest-path distance on a 10-pixel grid around the reeds.

Everything runs in this page in plain JavaScript, with no libraries. The test numbers quoted above come from running the same code without graphics; your runs will differ a little, since every run starts from random numbers. The Lab 3 brain is saved in your browser, so a reload does not throw away its training.
